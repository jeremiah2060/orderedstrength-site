What actually gets heavier

Everything else on this site is in service of one number going up.

We spend most of these pages proving that Jerry is honest. Honesty is not the product. It is what makes the product work, because a coach who flatters you cannot make you strong. This page is the other half: what happens to your body, and the one claim we refuse to make until we have earned it.

Three time scales

Strength is built in a set, a week and a block, and he works all three.

Inside one set

He watches the reps land, then writes the next one.

You finish a set and tell him how many reps you had left. He holds that against what he predicted you would do, and from the second set of the exercise onward the comparison moves the next one: down by as much as fifteen percent if the set came apart, up by at most five if it did not. The lopsidedness is deliberate, because he is quicker to protect a session than to push it, and the set that builds muscle is the one taken close enough to failure to be worth taking without being so far past it that the days after pay for it. That line sits somewhere different every time you walk in, which is the one thing a program written on Sunday cannot know about a Thursday.

Across one week

Enough work to grow, and a stop before it becomes a hole.

Muscle comes from doing more than your body is used to and then recovering from it. Both halves are real work and the second is the one people skip, so Jerry counts what each muscle has taken across the week and cuts the session when the arithmetic says you are borrowing from next week rather than earning this one. You will get weeks where he asks for less than you came in willing to give. Asking for less is the same job as asking for more, done in the direction nobody thanks a coach for.

Across one block

The reps come first. Then the plate goes on.

The weight does not move because a calendar said week four. It moves when you have shown the reps at the weight you are already lifting: reach the top of the range, hold it there, and the plate goes on, after which the rep target drops back to the middle of the range and the climb starts again. That is double progression, the oldest and least fashionable idea in the building. The number you watch while it happens is your estimated one rep max, and the word estimated is carrying real weight there. Nobody measured it under a bar. It is computed from the loads and reps you logged, it moves when they move, and Jerry prints it with that label on the screen for the same reason it is written that way here.

The claim we will not make

There is no before and after photograph on this site.

Results data none yet

We do not have a results dataset. The test group has not finished a full block, so any number we printed here about how much stronger anyone got would be invented. Inventing that number is the single most common thing in this category, and it is the exact behaviour the rest of this site exists to refuse.

When the data exists it goes on the record, next to the accuracy figures, and it will include the athletes it did not work for, because a results page containing only successes is an advertisement wearing a lab coat. Until then the promise is narrower and it is one that holds on your first session: every load he asks of you is written down before you lift it, with the reason attached, and you can hold him to it afterwards. If he is wrong about your body he tells you, rather than quietly moving the target and hoping you were not watching.

Where to start

Train for a block and check his work.

The fastest way to find out whether any of this is true for your body is to log real sets and read what he wrote down before each one. Read how it works for the loop itself, or take a receipt from your own phone to the verifier and check that nothing moved after the fact.
